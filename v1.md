# PacePay

**HackUTD 2026 · V1 product proposal · Open for discussion**  
**Working product name:** PacePay  
**Judging targets:** Overall, PNC, and NVIDIA, subject to event eligibility

## The idea

**PacePay finds payment terms you can actually afford and asks the business if they’ll accept them.**

You need your car fixed before your next shift. The shop wants $650. Rent and your car payment are due before payday. You have enough money in checking to pay the shop today, but doing that leaves you short when the other bills clear.

Take a picture of the quote. PacePay looks at your checking, savings, upcoming bills, and paycheck. It shows what happens if you pay the quote as written, how much you can safely offer today, and when you can afford the remainder. You choose the limits PacePay has to respect. With your permission, it asks the shop for different terms and checks whatever the shop comes back with.

The repair is our first use case. The broader idea applies any time an urgent bill and the timing of someone’s income are at odds.

## What makes it worth building

The most interesting question is **“What would have to change for this to work?”** PacePay answers with an exact amount or date and shows the obligation that determines it. Then it uses that answer to ask for different terms.

A customer should be able to see three things at a glance:

- **What breaks:** Paying in full now puts checking at −$300 before payday.
- **What works:** Paying $250 today and $400 after Monday’s paycheck keeps at least $100 in checking in our demo.
- **What to do next:** Ask the shop for those terms. Review the shop’s reply and approve the final schedule yourself.

The customer’s financial picture stays private. The shop sees the proposed amounts and dates. The customer sees the reasoning, any fees, and the merchant’s confirmation.

## The demo we want judges to remember

> “You’re the repair shop. Change the terms however you want.”

1. **Show the original quote and the next few days of the household’s finances.** The synthetic household has $1,650 in checking. $900 rent and a $400 car payment clear before Monday’s $1,000 paycheck. The customer wants to keep at least $100 in checking and has marked emergency savings off-limits.
2. **Show the decision.** Paying $650 upfront leaves checking at −$300 before payday. Moving money from savings would cross the customer’s chosen boundary. Paying $250 upfront leaves exactly $100 after the scheduled bills. The other $400 can be paid after Monday’s paycheck clears, given the obligations in this scenario.
3. **Hand the judge the merchant side.** They can say or type, “$250 today is fine, but I need the other $400 Sunday.” The new installment appears on the timeline. Checking drops to −$300 before payday. PacePay asks for Monday and shows the customer why Sunday failed.
4. **Let the judge change the deal again.** They might say, “Monday works with a $40 fee.” If the customer authorized up to $25 in fees, PacePay shows the fee limit separately from affordability and asks whether the shop can accept $25 or less. If the shop insists on terms outside the customer’s limits and no allowed option works, the app explains what would have to change.
5. **Show the outcome on both screens.** When there’s agreement, both sides see the same proposed payment schedule with amounts, dates, and fees. The customer sees the merchant’s confirmation and approves the plan. The household, merchant, and payments are simulated. If we demo voice, the call itself is live and uses a team-controlled phone.

The phone call earns its place if it lets a judge speak naturally and see the consequence while they’re still in the conversation. The same interaction should work through a live merchant page. The best visual is the offer changing next to the balance timeline, with the exact date or rule that changed the answer highlighted.

## Why it fits the tracks

**Overall.** Start with a problem anyone can understand in five seconds. Give the judge control over the outcome. End with a concrete proposal they helped shape. The demo should hold up when the judge gives us an offer we haven’t seen.

**PNC.** Checking tells us what’s available now. Scheduled bills and income tell us what will be available later. Savings gives the customer another option, subject to the limits they set. Those pieces come together in one decision the customer can understand and act on.

**NVIDIA.** Nemotron handles a consequential part of the workflow. It interprets the merchant’s reply, calls the tool that checks the offer, and uses the result to counter, ask for clarification, or report that no workable deal exists. The financial engine calculates and verifies the amounts and dates. The demo should make that handoff visible. Confirm the sponsor’s mixed-model policy before finalizing model choices.

## What V1 needs to prove

- A photographed estimate becomes a proposal the customer can verify against the original.
- A change in the deposit, deadline, or fee changes the result for the right reason.
- An offer can pass the upfront check and still fail on the second installment.
- An affordable offer can still exceed what the customer authorized.
- An ambiguous reply triggers a clarifying question.
- A hard refusal produces an honest “no workable deal” outcome.
- A stranger can explain what changed and why after seeing the demo once.

## Questions for the team

1. **Would you remember this after watching 20 demos?** Name the one moment you’d remember. If you can’t, propose a better one.
2. **Is the repair story the strongest use case?** A replacement should have a clearer deadline, a more believable negotiation, or a stronger reason the whole financial picture matters.
3. **What should the judge be allowed to change?** Pick the input that best shows the product adapting live.
4. **What would make the product feel credible?** Identify one detail that earns trust and one claim we should avoid making in the pitch.
5. **What should we cut?** Keep the smallest version that still delivers the full payoff.

For anyone reviewing this with Claude or Codex, ask for a blunt verdict, the strongest reason a judge might pass on it, one concrete change that improves the live demo, and one thing to cut. Bring those four answers back to the team.
